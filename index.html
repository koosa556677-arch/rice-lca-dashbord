Emission Hotspot #1: Field Methane

Continuous flooded paddy fields generate anaerobic conditions, causing ~50% of total GHG emissions in the form of methane ($CH_4$). Adopting Alternate Wetting & Drying (AWD) is the main lever for this stage (assumed −35% CH₄; see Methodology & Limitations).

Emission Hotspot #2: Straw Burning

Open field burning of rice straw (69% burning ratio in Central region) contributes ~39% of the regional total, about 4.28M tCO₂e in this model. This depends heavily on the straw-burning emission factor (1.12 kg CO₂e/kg), which appears to include CO₂ and should be verified. Under IPCC guidance biogenic CO₂ is normally excluded (see Methodology & Limitations). Switching to incorporation or bio-char removes burning emissions but incorporated straw can raise field CH₄, so net savings are smaller than the burning figure alone.

Yield Efficiency Impact

High-yielding varieties like Suphan Buri 1 (736 kg/rai) dilute fixed land emissions to achieve 1.76 kg CO₂e/kg, whereas lower-yield varieties like RD6 (357 kg/rai) rise to 3.63 kg CO₂e/kg.

Methodology & Limitations

Scope

  • Functional unit: 1 kg paddy rice. Boundary: cradle-to-farm-gate, Central Region, main-season rice (นาปี) crop year 2567/68 (2024/25).
  • Included: field CH₄, rice-straw burning, fertilizer, diesel. Excluded: pesticides, seed, irrigation electricity, organic inputs, post-harvest.
  • Method: IPCC framework with Thailand's Tier 2 seasonal CH₄ factor for irrigated main-season (นาปี) rice, Central region (143 kg CH₄/ha/season, to be verified against Thailand's BTR1), GWP-100 CH₄ = 28 (AR5-based); TGO emission factors for fertilizer and diesel. Main season only.
  • Status: screening LCA from public data. Not verified or certified under ISO 14067.

Known limitations

  • CH₄, straw burning, fertilizer and diesel per rai are identical for every variety (CH₄ about 641 kg CO₂e/rai). Differences in carbon footprint between varieties come only from yield. The Rice Department recommends lower fertilizer rates for photoperiod-sensitive varieties, so their footprint may be overstated here.
  • Field CH₄ converts area from rai to hectares (1 rai = 0.16 ha) before applying the seasonal factor. Earlier versions omitted this and overstated CH₄ about 6.25×.
  • The straw-burning factor (1.12 kg CO₂e/kg) appears to include biogenic CO₂; IPCC Tier 1 CH₄+N₂O only would be roughly 0.09–0.10 kg CO₂e/kg dry matter. This strongly affects the burning share. Verify before reporting.
  • The CH₄ factor is a single national seasonal value; it does not reflect cultivar duration, water management or straw management on individual farms.
  • The "Overall" row differs slightly from the sum of varieties (about 0.9% in total emissions).
  • Scenarios are simplified: AWD is an assumed −35% CH₄; zero burning does not add CH₄ from incorporated straw.
  • No uncertainty range is shown. Treat results as relative comparisons and hotspot identification, not precise values.
